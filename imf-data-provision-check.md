# China's fiscal data against the IMF 2026 Operational Guidance Note

*Verified 2026-10-19. Checks China's published fiscal data against the requirements of the current operational standard only. Every line names the Chinese data page that satisfies it, or records that none was found.*

---

## 1. What is being checked, and what is not

**The standard.** *Data Provision to the Fund for Surveillance Purposes — Operational Guidance Note*, IMF Policy Paper No. **2026/001**, 16 January 2026, 67 pages. Its ¶2: *"This note supersedes the 2013 operational guidance note on data provision to the Fund for surveillance purposes."*

- Landing page — https://www.imf.org/en/publications/policy-papers/issues/2026/01/16/data-provision-to-the-fund-for-surveillance-purposed-operational-guidance-note-573266
- **Full text, unblocked, tables rendered as HTML** — https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/007/2026/001/article-A001-en.xml

*(The IMF's own landing page, PDF metadata and URL slug read "Purposed"; the document header reads "Purposes.")*

**Scope.** This version checks **only** against the guidance note. An earlier draft also scored China against the SDDS dissemination standard and against the Article IV data-adequacy rating. Both are out: the SDDS is a voluntary publication subscription, and the adequacy rating is a staff judgment, not an obligation. Neither can produce a breach. They appear below only where they would otherwise be confused with the obligation.

**The two tests.** For each required item the note sets a **frequency** and a **time lag**. The obligation is to *provide* data to the Fund — and ¶31 accepts **publication on a government website** as one of four valid ways of discharging it, provided staff can access it and an understanding is in place. So publication is not merely a proxy here; for a published series it can be the provision itself. What cannot be checked from outside is whether something *not* published was supplied privately. Where publication clears the bar, provision is very likely to as well. Where it does not, the item is recorded as unverifiable, not as a breach.

**One carve-out to keep in mind.** Appendix VII ¶1: *"The methodologies identified as the basis for the general understandings are not the only acceptable methodologies; countries would still meet their obligation if specific understandings are reached on the use of older international compilation manuals or sound national methodology, where adequate for surveillance."*

## 2. Item 4 — the standing fiscal obligation

The note publishes, for the first time in an operational Fund document, the general understandings for the pre-2024 Annex A items. Appendix VII Table 1, fiscal row, verbatim:

> **Frequency:** *"Quarterly for central government. Annually for general government and debt stocks"*
> **Time lag:** *"Three months for central government. Three quarters for general government and debt stocks."*
> **Methodology:** *"Government Finance Statistics Manual 2014 and Quarterly Government Finance Statistics-Guide for Compilers and Users."*

Note the grouping, which is easy to get wrong: **central-government debt stocks are annual on a three-quarter lag**, bracketed with general government, not with central-government flows. A two-quarter figure for central government does appear in the Fund's surveillance table, but that is a dissemination benchmark, not this obligation.

Reference period for the annual items is calendar 2025, ending 2025-12-31. Three quarters runs to about 2026-09-30; three months, for the quarterly item, to about 91 days after each quarter end.

#### 2.3.1 Line 12 — central government guarantees

**Nothing current is published.** No guaranteed-debt line appears on the NSDP, in the 2025 final-accounts debt table or its explanatory note, in the State Council's 2024 or 2025 debt reports, or in China's IMF data-standards metadata for central government debt. China reports central government only to the World Bank's quarterly public-sector debt database, with no guaranteed line. The IMF's surveillance table does carry a row titled "Stocks of Central Government and Central Government-Guaranteed Debt", but that is the Fund's standard row label, not evidence of a separate series.

**The last official figure is thirteen years old.** The National Audit Office's announcement No. 32 of 2013 put central government 负有担保责任的债务 — debt for which it bears guarantee responsibility — at **2,600.72 亿** as of end-June 2013. Of that, 1,090 亿 were Central Huijin bonds *"由财政部提供担保"*, guaranteed by the Ministry of Finance. A 20-year Huijin tranche issued in 2010 would not mature until around 2030, so part of that stock may still be outstanding; whether the guarantee still applies could not be confirmed. *(The original audit.gov.cn page now returns 404; the text was read from a government mirror at panzhihua.gov.cn.)*

**The law does not make the stock nil, so a nil return is not available.** The Budget Law's Article 35 bans guarantees by *local* government only: *"地方政府及其所属部门不得为任何单位和个人的债务以任何方式提供担保"*. Article 34, on central borrowing, contains no equivalent ban. The Supreme People's Court interpretation 法释〔2020〕28号, Article 5, voids guarantees by state organs *"但是经国务院批准为使用外国政府或者国际经济组织贷款进行转贷的除外"* — except State-Council-approved on-lending of foreign-government and international-organisation loans. So central guarantees are legally confined to that on-lending category and to State Council approvals: plausibly small, but not nil.

**A related international series exists but does not substitute.** The World Bank's International Debt Statistics carry "private debt guaranteed by the public sector" (series DT.DOD.PRPG.CD) for China at US$25.86bn in 2024, up from US$17.14bn in 2021 and about zero before. The guarantor there is any public entity, not necessarily central government, so it cannot stand in for line 12.

**A related coverage caveat in the final accounts.** Footnote 2 of the 2025 central debt table states that certain other repayable central liabilities — bank-related debts and borrowing by departments — sit outside the reported stock until *"规范管理后纳入"*, brought in once their management is standardised. That is not a guarantee line, but it is a stated gap in the central perimeter.

**Verdict: not met by publication.** China may well report this to the Fund privately; the IMF's surveillance table records the combined row as received quarterly. But no public source supports either a figure or a nil.

## 3. Items 13 to 15 — the obligations added in 2024

Appendix VII Table 2 unpacks the three Annex A lines into eight reporting lines, all annual. Item 13's headline carries a three-quarter lag; items 14 and 15 carry three quarters for general government and two quarters for central government.

### 3.1 Item 13 — total stock of general government debt, first reporting period **2025**

Definition, verbatim: *"Total stock of debt … refers to total gross debt consisting of all liabilities that are debt instruments … SDRs; Currency and deposits; Debt securities; Loans; Insurance, pension, and standardized guarantee schemes; Other accounts payable."* Perimeter is general government per the 2004 Decision: central government including extrabudgetary and social security funds, plus state and local governments.

**Item 13 is met, three times over.** Four independent checks hold together. The consolidated total of 96 万亿 in the State Council report matches both component sums, 96.05 万亿 from March and from July. The report's stated debt ratio of 68.5% reproduces exactly from its own 2025 GDP figure of 140.2 万亿. And the central-government closing stock in the final accounts satisfies its own flow identity: opening 345,723.62 plus issuance 161,409.02 less repayment 94,874.34 gives the domestic part exactly; the 59.38 亿 residual sits entirely on the external part and is foreign-currency revaluation.

**The State Council report also publishes what the IMF's augmented measure is reaching for.** Including hidden debt, *"全国政府债务（含法定债务和隐性债务）余额102.5万亿元"*, a ratio of 73.2%. Hidden debt itself fell from 10.5 万亿 at end-2024 to **6.5 万亿** at end-2025, down 38%, largely through the swap programme. This is the second consecutive year China has published a hidden-debt figure.

**A preliminary-to-final revision is visible and worth knowing.** The March budget report gave local general debt as 175,120.36 and special debt as 373,110.46; the July final accounts give 175,123.66 and 373,108.30. The net revision is about 1 亿 on a 96 万亿 stock. The March figure is preliminary, the July one final.

**One definitional caveat, stated for completeness.** China's total is *statutory* debt — debt securities and loans. Item 13's definition, *"all liabilities that are debt instruments"*, also includes other accounts payable. Government arrears to enterprises are being cleared under a national campaign, and Hunan's 2026 adjustment report alone records 196 亿 of special bonds for 化解拖欠企业账款, but no national stock of government arrears is published. The note's preference for GFSM 2014 is stated as *"preferably"*, so this is a coverage gap in the measure rather than a missing submission.

**Three of item 15's four instruments are now accounted for.** Deposits are published, monthly for treasury funds and annually for social funds. Gold and SDRs are a nil for general government, which is itself a valid answer. **The genuine gap is the government's holdings of debt securities**, which no public source breaks out.

**The data almost certainly exists internally.** The Ministry of Finance compiles an accrual-basis *government comprehensive financial report* (政府综合财务报告); the 2025 final-accounts report of June 2026 instructs that the national report continue to be compiled, but it is not published. That report is the likeliest source from which China could supply item 15 by 2027. The fund accounting rules (财会〔2017〕28号) already require each social insurance fund to keep asset accounts for bond investments and entrusted investments, so the instrument split exists at fund level.

### 3.3 The 80% coverage rule, for when it bites in 2027

Denominator is total gross debt at the relevant level of government **excluding guaranteed debt**, tested separately for central and general government, **each reporting year**. The uncovered remainder is not a free pass: the member must still state what instrument types or which issuing government units it consists of.

China is unusually well placed here. It already publishes local government debt by province monthly, and the provincial statutory filings give debt limits and balances by issuing unit — precisely the residual disclosure the rule asks for.

## 5. The perimeter question, settled by silence

A full-text search of the 67-page note returns **zero** occurrences of "public corporation," "nonfinancial public," "non-financial public," "state-owned," "SOE," "public sector debt" or "financing vehicle."

The mandatory perimeter is stated three times as general government per the 2004 Decision: central government including budgetary, extrabudgetary and social security funds, plus state and local governments. Appendix VII Table 2 is headed "Mandatory **Public Sector** Indicators," but every line inside it is defined at central or general government level only — the title is broader than the content.

The only adjacent language in the whole note sits in the **data adequacy questionnaire**, which is the staff judgment and not the obligation: *"Is the coverage of government operations and debt statistics sufficiently comprehensive … including risks from quasi-fiscal activities?"*

**So local government financing vehicles are outside the mandatory perimeter** unless an individual entity is classified inside general government under sector-classification rules — and the note gives no guidance on making that call. Combined with ¶26, which bars staff from being the source of the data, this means the Fund's augmented debt measure cannot generate a compliance finding against China, however large the gap it describes.

## 6. What an outside check cannot reach

Listed so they are not read as findings either way.

1. **Provision itself is not public.** Everything here tests publication, which ¶31 accepts as a manner of provision. What cannot be checked is whether anything *not* published was supplied privately.
2. **Line 12, guaranteed debt.** No current stock and no nil statement anywhere; last official figure 2013. China may report it privately — the IMF's surveillance table records the combined row as received quarterly — but no public source supports a figure or a nil.
3. **Line 6, the non-deposit part of net acquisition of financial assets.** Deposits are published monthly; government purchases of securities, loans extended and equity are not. China reports on the GFSM 1986 framework, which has no such line.
4. **Item 15, government holdings of debt securities** — due 2027. Buried in the social funds' financial assets without an instrument split. Likely available internally in MOF's unpublished accrual-basis government financial report.
5. **Item 14, the foreign official/private split of non-resident bond holdings.** Not published by any source. Immaterial to compliance, since the domestic buckets already clear the 80% rule.
6. **Specific understandings and the capacity defence** (lines 20 and 21). Neither is published per country, so China's actual benchmark may differ from the general understandings used here.
7. **The monitoring record.** Per footnote 38, the fuller compliance record for items 13 to 15 goes to an internal platform *"currently under development."*

**Nothing in this check amounts to a finding of breach.** Of the 11 requirements that bear on China now — item 4 and item 13 — 9 are fully met by publication, most of them with large margins, and one is partly met. The single requirement not met by any public source is central-government-guaranteed debt. Items 14 and 15 are not due until 2027.

**Every line once marked unverifiable has now been resolved one way or the other.** Evidence came from six independent public sources: the Ministry of Finance's final accounts and budget report, the National Bureau of Statistics' National Summary Data Page, the State Council's debt report to the legislature, the central bank's monetary statistics, the central depository's holder tables, and the ministry's foreign-loan table.

**The item I previously called the real exposure is met.** Item 13, the 2025 general-government debt total, was stated as a single figure — 96 万亿 — in the State Council's report of 25 August 2026, and visible in components from 5 March.

**The tightest line is general-government flows.** China cleared a three-quarter allowance by fourteen days, in a regime where late provision is treated as nonprovision. If the 2026 final accounts slip by more than two weeks relative to this year's timing, that margin is gone.

**The genuine gaps are narrow.** Guaranteed debt has not been published since 2013. Government holdings of debt securities, needed for item 15 from 2027, are not broken out. Both are the kind of data China likely holds internally — the first in its debt-management records, the second in its unpublished accrual-basis government financial report.

**Two corrections made during this check**, recorded so the reasoning can be audited. The final-accounts debt tables were first read with their 2024 opening balances taken as 2025 figures; reading them column by column fixed it before any wrong number reached the document. And the 20% creditor trigger was first tested on a place-of-issue measure described as covering every foreign creditor; it is now tested on the official-creditor measure the note specifies, and on the strictest residency reading, and is not met on either.
